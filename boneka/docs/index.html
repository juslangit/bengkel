Boneka

boneka ("puppet") is a local app where Luqman types what he wants, watches Blender build it part by part, presses one button to rig it, and then types how it should move. It is a personal tool, built for him rather than for anyone else, and it runs entirely on his own machine at no cost: system Python, the Blender that is already installed, and three.js kept locally. Meshy is available behind a button that always asks first, because credits are real money.

Why this project exists

Luqman asked for an app he could prompt to create a 3D asset in real time, so he can see the process, then rig with one button, then animate by prompting again. The three parts matter in that order. Watching the model appear is not decoration: it is the difference between a black box that hands over a file and a tool whose working he can follow, which is the lesson from the Blender add-on of 2026-09-05.

The gap it fills is real and was left deliberately open elsewhere. gudang, his asset receiving bay, states in its own scope that characters, rigging and animation authoring are out of scope. gudang judges and prepares assets that arrive; boneka makes them and gives them a skeleton. They do not overlap, which is the mistake that killed kilang and claude-terminal.

What counts as "done"

  • done A prompt becomes a model, drawn part by part in the browser as Blender makes it
  • done Each character is assembled from named pieces — body, then face, then clothing, armour and gear — each tied to its own bone
  • done The parts are fused into one sculpted form rather than left as an assembly
  • done Proportions come from named references, and checks hold them there
  • done Photographs of the subject appear beside the model while it builds
  • done A named palette recolours the whole model, shades included
  • done Every surface is textured by what it is made of
  • done Full materials — normal, roughness and metallic — and a lit environment
  • to do A face. Attempted three times and recorded as a limit of the method (D-024)
  • done One button rigs it, with bones placed from the recipe rather than guessed
  • done A second prompt animates it, and it plays back in the viewport
  • done Export to .glb, .fbx and .blend with the animation baked
  • done Motion capture .fbx clips can be dropped in and retargeted
  • done Meshy available, never automatic, always priced first
  • done Checks that drive the real Blender, not mocks — 621 of them
  • to do Luqman has actually opened it and made something — M7, and the only open item
  • to do An asset boneka made is used in one of his real games

What is NOT in scope

  • Any neural network for geometry. The recipes are hand-written proportions; Meshy is the escape hatch when that is not enough (D-002)
  • PBR material authoring: normal, roughness and metallic maps. Colour and a detail multiply only
  • Sculpting by hand. The pass is procedural: remesh, relax, smooth. Blender is the right place to push vertices around, and the .blend export opens there
  • Facial rigging, fingers, IK controls and constraint rigs — FK bones only (D-005)
  • Editing the mesh by hand in the browser. boneka builds and re-builds; Blender edits
  • Anything hosted, shared or deployed. This is a tool on one Mac (D-006)
  • Judging, versioning or exporting other people's assets — that is gudang

01-idea.md · 3 sections

The idea

On 2026-09-20 Luqman asked for:

"an app that i can prompt to create a 3D assets (u create it) real time so i can see the process and i can press a button to auto rig the assets. then i can prompt to make animation to it"

Three verbs in one sentence — make, rig, animate — and one qualifier that shaped everything: real time so i can see the process. He did not ask for a fast generator. He asked to watch.

The four decisions taken before a line was written

He was asked four questions and chose:

QuestionHis answer
How the model gets madeBlender script, with Meshy on a button. The prompt becomes geometry built step by step; Meshy stays behind an explicit press
How the rig button worksBones placed and fitted automatically in Blender, offline, scripted
Where the motion comes fromBoth — procedural keyframes now, an .fbx library he can drop clips into as well
What the app looks likeA local web app — chat on the left, live viewport on the right

Three of the four were the recommendation. On animation he chose the wider option, which is why animations/ and the retargeter exist in v1 rather than being left for later.

The insight the whole thing rests on

Auto-rigging a downloaded model is hard because the rigger is handed a cloud of triangles and has to guess where the elbow is. boneka does not have that problem, because boneka built the mesh. When the recipe places the left forearm it also records where that bone starts and ends. The rig button then places bones from the recipe: exact, every time, on a dog as readily as on a person.

That single fact is what makes a one-press rig honest rather than a coin toss, and it is why the model is built from named parts instead of being sculpted as one lump. The guessing route still exists — it is what runs on a Meshy import — but it is the fallback, not the main road.

Alternatives considered

  • Meshy for everything. Best-looking results, but every prompt costs credits, takes one to three minutes, and "real time" would have meant a progress bar rather than watching. Rejected as the default, kept as a button (D-002).
  • A Blender add-on panel instead of a browser. Rejected by him. The add-on of 2026-09-05 is the reason: a thing that lives inside Blender is a thing he has to be inside Blender to use, and he could not explain that one afterwards.
  • Mixamo for rigging and animation. The best quality available and free, but it has no public API — every model would need a manual browser upload and download, so the button could never be one press. Its clips are still welcome: that is what animations/ is for.

02-planning.md · 4 sections

Planning

The pipeline

   prompt
     |
     v
 recipes.py     words -> a build plan: ~25 named parts, each with its size,
     |          place, colour, and the bone it makes or rides on
     v
 build.py       plan -> Blender geometry, one part at a time.
     |          Each finished part is exported on its own as a small .glb
     |          and pushed to the browser  <-- this is "watching the process"
     v
 rig.py         the parts already know their bones, so the skeleton is placed
     |          from the recipe. Rigid weights first, then softened at joints
     v
 anim.py        a move -> keyframes on the pose bones, written as world-space
     |          turns and converted into bone space at the last moment
     v
 .glb / .fbx / .blend

Everything runs inside one Blender that is started once and left running. Commands go in on stdin as JSON, events come back on stdout. The scene, the rig and the pose survive between button presses, which is why Auto-Rig is instant.

Second pass, 2026-09-20 — reference and sculpting

Luqman looked at the first day's output and said "the assets is so blocky, search reference before modelling and use sculpting also". Both halves were taken as standing instructions, not as a note about this one model.

Reference. Every creature was rebuilt to a named source — the eight-head canon for people, breed-standard length-against-height for dogs, measured dimensions for a chicken — with the source written in the code beside the numbers and checks holding the geometry to them. The app does the same for him: a prompt fetches photographs of its subject from Wikimedia Commons into a panel beside the viewport.

Sculpting. The soft parts are now voxel-remeshed into one continuous skin, relaxed and shaded smooth, so the seam where an arm meets a shoulder stops being a seam. Colour survives by grouping, the exact rig survives by transferring the weights across, and thin things survive by being measured and left alone. Machines, crates and held weapons stay crisp on purpose.

673 checks, still green, now including the proportions themselves.

Where it stands, 2026-09-20

Everything in the loop works and is checked. 621 checks pass in about 20 seconds, and they drive the real Blender rather than mocking it. Six models were rendered and looked at, a walk cycle was rendered frame by frame to confirm the mesh deforms correctly, and the interface was screenshotted with a model loaded, skeleton overlay on, animation playing.

What the checks caught, which is the argument for having written them: a crystal and a rocket both half underground, a chicken whose beak pointed backwards, a snowman's carrot nose pointing backwards too, and an axe whose blade faced its own handle.

What eyes caught that checks could not: hips 72 cm across (a mixed-up size convention), a sword buried inside the arm that held it, a dog whose head was swallowed by its own neck, and a robot wearing gold trousers.

Four more rounds of rendering-and-looking went into the second pass, and what they caught is the argument for doing it: limbs deflated by too much smoothing, a crease at the waist where two masses met without overlapping, a cape that was still a flat plank, a chicken's tail standing off its back as a separate card, and a 20 cm bird being remeshed at 1.8 mm because the voxel bounds were the wrong way round. No check would have objected to any of them.

Third pass: "still blocky, make it more natural"

Luqman looked again and said the models were still blocky. A silhouette render settled the argument in one picture: the torso was a box — a rounded slab with square shoulder corners, arms welded to its sides and two identical sticks for legs. Smoothing a box gives a smooth box; the problem was upstream of the sculpt pass, in what the parts were made of.

So a new primitive: loft(), which describes a part by its cross-sections. A torso is now a stack of rings — wide at the shoulders, narrow at the waist, wide again at the hips; an arm is a shoulder ring, a bicep swell, an elbow, a forearm swell and a wrist. Widths came from anthropometry rather than the drawing canon, because the canon measures the fleshed silhouette and this has to build the flesh. Deltoids cap the shoulders, a shoulder blade and a haunch carry a dog's legs into its body, and the figure stands in a slight A-pose so there is air between arm and waist.

Four rounds of silhouette-and-fix: legs floating clear of the ground, a shoulder mass that was itself the slab, an upper-leg ring punching a plate through the flank, and one tiny ear dragging the whole model's voxel size down to a millimetre. 673 checks, still green.

Fourth pass: modular characters

"when i ask to make a 'knight' i want u to create the model 1 by 1 as a modular model, for example u create a base body first, then u make the helmet, then u make the armor and so on."

Until then a knight was a blue person holding a sword. Now each kind of character has a kit — an ordered list of the pieces it is assembled from — and each piece is its own part, fitted to the body underneath and tied to the bone it should move with. Twenty-six modules, kits for about thirty characters, and a plain person still gets dressed.

The build now names the pass it is on, so the order is visible: Body, Face, Armour, Clothing, Gear. An unpainted character wears its kit's own colours — a knight in steel, a ninja in black, a king in red and gold — while a named colour still wins.

722 checks, including that a knight has a helmet and a golem wears nothing.

Fifth pass: palettes

Luqman asked whether boneka had access to different colour palettes. It had thirty-five colour words and shades made by multiplying, which is why the dark under-layer often looked muddy. Now a prompt can name a palette and the whole model is built from it — ten moods offline, or anything on Lospec, cached after the first fetch.

The interesting part was the snapping. Colours must stay distinct or the model goes flat, and must stay in lightness order or the shading inverts. Two greedy versions failed before it was done properly as an ordered assignment solved with a small dynamic program, which makes both true by construction.

765 checks.

Sixth pass: textures

Luqman pasted texturelabs.org and said to take it as the texture library. Every part now gets a surface from what it is, multiplied over its colour so the palette still rules. A texturelabs CLI went on the PATH alongside polyhaven and sfx, since the library is useful outside boneka too.

The licence needed reading properly before building: free and commercial-safe, but not redistributable and specifically not to be shipped inside a 3D model where someone could extract it. Every textured export now writes a note beside itself saying so.

779 checks.

Seventh pass: materials, light, and a limit found

"i dont want basic shape, do complex modelling until the output become realistic." The ceiling was stated up front — a recipe cannot make a face — and then the headroom below it was spent, materials first.

Normal and roughness maps are now derived from each Texturelabs photograph and the viewport is lit by a Poly Haven HDRI instead of three lamps in a void. That pair changed the output more than any geometry work would have in the same time: armour became metal, a rock became carved stone. Hands got five fingers. Garments were let into the sculpt pass so they fuse into continuous clothing instead of ending in flat discs in mid-air.

The face was attempted three times and photographed each time, and the result is recorded as a limit rather than a to-do (D-024). What is there now is a profile, not a likeness.

783 checks.

It has never been used by a person. That is the only open item that matters.

04-methods.md · 15 sections

Methods

Running it
boneka                 # starts the server and opens the browser
boneka --no-browser    # for when you just want the server

boneka is on the PATH as a symlink in ~/.local/bin pointing at ~/Desktop/project/3d/bengkel/boneka/boneka. Stop it with Ctrl-C; that closes Blender too. If the port is busy it takes the next one and says so, rather than dying on a stack trace.

Environment: BONEKA_BLENDER if Blender moves, BONEKA_PORT to change the starting port.

Driving Blender without the app

The worker speaks JSON lines, so anything can talk to it — including a shell pipe, which is how most of this was developed and tested:

cd ~/Desktop/project/3d/bengkel/boneka
printf '%s\n' \
  '{"cmd":"build","prompt":"a tall blue knight with a sword"}' \
  '{"cmd":"rig"}' \
  '{"cmd":"animate","prompt":"walk"}' \
  '{"cmd":"export","format":"glb"}' \
| /Applications/Blender.app/Contents/MacOS/Blender --background --factory-startup \
    --python blender/worker.py -- sessions/scratch 2>/dev/null | grep '@@BK@@'

Commands: build, rig, animate, clip, rest, import, export, ping. Events come back marked @@BK@@ so Blender's own chatter can be filtered out.

The checks
python3 tests/check.py               # 621 checks, ~20s, drives real Blender
python3 tests/check.py --quick       # six models instead of twelve
python3 tests/check.py --parser-only # no Blender needed at all

Run the full one before committing. It builds, rigs, animates and exports every archetype, tries the fitted-rig path on an imported model, and confirms that animating before rigging gives a polite message rather than a crash.

Look it up first

Before writing or changing a recipe, find a reference and take the numbers from it. Every archetype in recipes.py carries its source in the docstring — the eight-head canon, breed-standard dog ratios, measured chicken dimensions — and the checks hold the geometry to them. Do not adjust a proportion because it looks better on screen without finding out what the real ratio is; if the reference says one thing and the render says another, the render is telling you about the sculpt pass, not the anatomy.

The two rules that keep the maths straight

Sizes are half-extents. Half the width, half the depth, half the height — or the radius, for anything round. Everywhere, no exceptions. The first draft mixed half and full sizes and produced a person with 72 cm hips.

Poses are written in world space. "Swing the right thigh forward twenty degrees" is ("X", radians(-20)), and _world_to_local converts it into the bone's own space at the last moment. Never write a pose in bone-local terms: it will depend on how that bone happens to be rolled, and that is the usual reason hand-written rig animation comes out twisted.

Building a part from cross-sections

loft(part, rings, colour, bone, label) is the main primitive for anything organic. Each ring is {"c": [x, y, z], "rx": half-width, "ry": half-depth} and they run along the form. Rings are laid perpendicular to the path through their neighbours, so a curved stack gives a curved form.

Three rules, each learned by breaking it:

  • Overlap, do not abut. A remesh can only blend volumes that share space.
  • A limb's top ring must be narrower than the body it enters, or it shows as a plate through the flank. Put a blending mass there instead — a deltoid, a shoulder blade, a haunch.
  • Leave air under the arm. The gap between arm and waist is most of what makes a standing figure read as a person, so the rest pose is a slight A-pose.

Check the result with a silhouette render, not a lit one. Lighting hides a bad shape; a flat black shape against white does not. That is how the box torso, the square shoulders and the welded arms were all spotted at once.

Working on the sculpt pass

blender/sculpt.py fuses the soft parts into one skin. Three rules live in it and each exists because breaking it broke something:

  • Group by colour before remeshing. A remesh keeps one material.
  • Paint the vertex groups on first and transfer them back after. A remesh throws them away, and they are the exact rig.
  • Measure what is too thin and leave it alone. A voxel grid swallows anything under about two voxels.

Two masses must overlap to blend. Meeting at a plane leaves a crease — that is what the ridge at the dog's waist and the seam at the figure's navel were, and both were fixed by making the pelvis and ribcage taller rather than by smoothing harder. Smoothing harder deflates the whole model instead; six passes at 0.7 made everything look starved, and four at 0.55 with fuller limb radii is the balance that came out right.

Voxel size is min(slimmest_soft_part / 3, height / 70). The first bound keeps the hands; the second keeps the triangle count sane. Getting these the wrong way round made a 20 cm chicken remesh at 1.8 mm.

Textures

texturelabs is on the PATH — categories, search, list, get, pick, sync — and is the way to get a texture for anything, not just boneka. It writes a SOURCES.md beside what it downloads.

Inside boneka, server.py holds CURATED (which slug per surface) and STRENGTH (how hard each surface pushes). To add a surface:

  1. Look at the candidates first — texturelabs pick <category> -n 5 -o . and actually open them. The site mixes design overlays and decorative tilework in with the real surfaces, and a random pick will eventually put Moroccan tilework on a brick wall.
  2. Add the slug to CURATED and a range to STRENGTH. The range is what the grey map is squeezed into: (0.84, 1.05) is a whisper, (0.58, 1.10) is a shove.
  3. Add the part words to MATERIALS in recipes.py, or the prop to PROP_MATERIAL if its parts are named too generically to read.

Two traps, both already paid for:

  • Use ShaderNodeMix, never ShaderNodeMixRGB. Blender 5's glTF exporter reads the modern node as baseColorFactor × baseColorTexture and silently ignores the old one — which loses every colour and exports a model that is uniformly grey, with no error anywhere.
  • A prop word in a prompt is not the subject's material. "a knight with a sword" sets prop to sword, and for a while that made the knight's whole body metal.

Never commit textures/ or a _TEXTURES.txt export. See D-020.

Palettes

blender/palette.py holds the built-in moods and the snapping; the server resolves names and caches Lospec fetches into palettes/.

  • A mood is ten or so hex colours in MOODS. Order them dark to light; the snapping sorts them anyway, but a readable list is easier to judge.
  • The server resolves a name to colours and hands Blender the colours, not the name. Keep it that way: the worker must never make a network call, or a build can hang on a slow request.
  • If you touch snap_plan, the two properties to protect are distinct and in order. The checks assert both across every mood plus a 16-colour Lospec palette, and separately that a three-colour palette still does not invert the shading.

To see what a palette actually does, render the same prompt under several and put them side by side. Numbers will not tell you that a steel knight has turned into a teal one.

Adding a character, or a new piece of kit

A new kind of character is usually a list, not geometry. Add its word to ARCHETYPES["humanoid"] in recipes.py if it is not there, then a line in KITS naming the modules it is assembled from, and optionally a pair in KIT_COLOURS for when the prompt names no colour.

A genuinely new piece of kit is a function _o_yourthing(f) returning a list of parts, registered in MODULES. f is a Fit: it knows the body's height, build, palette and landmarks, and gives you f.r(x, y, z, rx, ry) for a ring, f.torso_rx(z) for the half-width of the torso at a height, f.head(t, grow) for a ring around the head, and arm_at(z) / leg_at(z) for where a limb is. Build everything as a function of those rather than as fixed numbers, or the piece will only fit the figure you tested it on.

Every module must:

  • attach= the bone it should move with — the pauldron to shoulder.L, the greave to shin.L, the helmet to head. The check "every piece hangs off a real bone" fails otherwise.
  • pass hard=True, so the sculpt pass leaves its edges alone.
  • carry a stage: clothing, armour or gear. That is what the app shows while it builds.
Adding a new prop
  1. Write _p_yourthing(s, pal, words, style, bulk) in blender/recipes.py, returning a list of step(...) calls. s is the size scale; sizes are half-extents; the thing must sit on z = 0.
  2. Add it to PROPS and to PROP_WORDS with the words that should reach it.
  3. Optionally add default colours to PROP_COLORS.
  4. python3 tests/check.py --parser-only — the sweep over every prop will tell you straight away if it is underground or has no steps.
  5. Render it and look at it (see below). Checks cannot see a shape.
Adding a new move
  1. Write _h_yourmove(p, a) in blender/anim.py, where p runs 0→1 across the cycle and a is the amount. Return {bone: [(axis, radians), ...]}, plus "_root": {"loc": (x, y, z), "rot": [...]} for whole-body motion.
  2. Add it to HUMANOID, and give QUADRUPED, BIRD and PROP something too — the check "every rig knows every move" will fail otherwise, and a button that does nothing is worse than no button.
  3. Add its words to MOVES and its length to BASE_FRAMES. If it plays once rather than looping, add it to ONE_SHOT.
Looking at what you made

Checks cannot see a shape, so render it. The throwaway script used during the build imports a .glb, lights it from three sides, frames it and renders with EEVEE — that is how the buried rocket and the backwards beak were found. Pass a frame number as a third argument to catch a model mid-animation, which is how the walk cycle was verified.

The lock on the door

Every /api/ request needs two things: the token minted at startup and carried in the address bar, and an Origin that is boneka's own (or absent, for a plain page load). This is not decoration. A localhost server with no lock is reachable by every page in the browser — the claude-terminal hole of 2026-09-13 was exactly this. Do not add an endpoint that skips _token_ok and _origin_ok.

Housekeeping

sessions/ fills up — one folder per run, holding every part snapshot. It is gitignored and safe to empty. animations/*.fbx is gitignored too: those clips are not ours to redistribute.

How it sits next to his other projects

gudang is the one to be careful about, and the line between them is already drawn in gudang's own notes: "Characters, rigging and animation authoring" are explicitly out of gudang's scope. gudang is the receiving bay — it inspects, judges, versions and exports assets that arrive from Sketchfab or Meshy. boneka is upstream of that: it makes an asset and gives it a skeleton. A boneka export is a perfectly good thing to hand to gudang.

They must not grow into each other. Two projects aimed at one need is one too many — that killed claude-terminal and folded kilang into gudang, and it is the standing lesson.

The games — kedai-runtuh, direct-hit, codebot, snapshot, red-card — are where a boneka asset would actually be used, and M8 says so. Godot reads .glb directly and the models come out at real-world scale, which is the thing that goes wrong with 16 of his 32 Sketchfab downloads.

Siti was to be the one agent that makes things for him, with 3D as one of her skills. She is paused for want of a paid API key. boneka reaches the same "make me a thing" goal by a route that needs no key at all, because the understanding is a hand-written parser rather than a model. If Siti ever restarts, boneka is a skill she could call rather than something to rebuild.

shorts-factory is the closest sibling in spirit: a personal local tool on one Mac, no git remote, no portfolio card. boneka differs on the last two — it is a git repo, and it is worth showing.

06-decisions.md · 27 sections

Decisions

D-001 — Build the mesh from named parts, not as one sculpted lump

2026-09-20. Every recipe places parts that know their own name and the bone they make or ride on.

Why: it is what makes a one-press auto-rig honest. A rigger handed a cloud of triangles has to guess where the elbow is; a rigger handed a part called forearm.L that records where its bone starts and ends does not. It also gives the build something to show part by part, which is the feature he actually asked for. The cost is that the models are assemblies of primitives rather than sculpted forms — accepted, because Meshy is there for when that is not enough.

D-002 — Meshy is a button, never automatic

2026-09-20. Every generation asks first and states the cost; the server refuses a request without confirm: true.

Why: his standing rule, and the same order kilang was given — build in Blender first, Meshy only when he says so. Credits are real money. The server enforces it rather than trusting the page, because the page is the easier thing to bypass.

D-003 — One Blender, started once, kept alive

2026-09-20. The server owns a single blender --background subprocess and speaks JSON lines to it.

Why: Auto-Rig has to feel like one press. Starting Blender per command would cost seconds each time and lose the scene, so the rig would have to reload the model it was about to rig. Keeping it alive means the scene, the skeleton and the pose are all still there between presses. Considered and rejected: a Blender add-on talking to the page, which would mean he has to have Blender open.

D-004 — Sizes are half-extents, everywhere

2026-09-20. Half the width, half the depth, half the height; the radius for anything round.

Why: the first draft used full dimensions in the creature recipes and half-extents in the prop library. The result was a person with hips 72 cm across. One rule, applied without exception, is the only thing that keeps this kind of error out — and fourteen numbers had to be corrected to get there.

D-005 — FK bones only, no IK and no control rig

2026-09-20. The armature is plain forward-kinematic bones in the build pose.

Why: every animation boneka writes is authored as bone rotations, so IK would be machinery with nothing driving it. It also exports cleanly: a .glb with an IK rig carries constraints that Godot and Unreal quietly drop. If he ever wants to pose by hand in Blender, the .blend export is the right place for it, and adding Rigify there is a separate job.

D-006 — Poses are authored in world space and converted at the last moment

2026-09-20. An animation says ("X", radians(-20)) meaning a turn about the world X axis; _world_to_local converts it through the bone's rest matrix.

Why: a bone pointing straight down has an ambiguous roll, and Blender resolves it by a rule that is easy to get wrong. Writing poses in bone-local terms makes every move depend on that resolution, which is why hand-written rig animation usually comes out twisted. Converting at the last moment means a pose reads the way it is described out loud and works on any rig.

D-007 — A token and an origin check on every request

2026-09-20. /api/ refuses anything without the per-run token and anything whose Origin is not boneka's own.

Why: localhost is not a security boundary. A server listening on 127.0.0.1 with no lock can be driven by any page in the browser — that was exactly the claude-terminal hole of 2026-09-13, where any website the user visited could run commands. A POST with Content-Type: text/plain is a CORS simple request and gets no preflight, so CORS alone protects nothing.

D-008 — Local only: no hosting, no deployment, no shared state

2026-09-20. boneka runs on one Mac and writes to one folder.

Why: it is a personal tool, it drives a desktop Blender, and it has no reason to exist anywhere else. It differs from shorts-factory in being a git repo and in being worth a portfolio card, but not in being hosted.

D-009 — Procedural motion now, a clip library alongside it

2026-09-20. Both routes ship in v1: 19 hand-written moves, and .fbx clips dropped into animations/ and retargeted.

Why: his choice when asked — he took the wider option over procedural-only. The procedural moves always work and cost nothing; the clips give real motion capture quality when he wants it. The retarget reads each turn in the source rig's own space and writes it into ours, so the two skeletons need only agree on which bone is which. The result is close rather than exact, and that is stated plainly in the README rather than hidden.

D-010 — Look the subject up before modelling it

2026-09-20. Every archetype's proportions are taken from a named reference and the source is written in the code beside the numbers: the eight-head canon for people, breed-standard length-against-height for dogs, measured dimensions for a chicken. Checks hold them there.

Why: Luqman's verdict on the first day's output was "the assets is so blocky, search reference before modelling and use sculpting also". The first version's proportions were invented, and invented proportions read as wrong immediately to someone who models for a living. The eight-head figure alone moved the crotch from 0.53 of height to 0.50, the knee from 0.28 to 0.26, the shoulders from 0.81 to 0.833 and the head from a sixth of the body to an eighth.

The app does the same thing for him: typing a prompt fetches photographs of the subject from Wikimedia Commons into a panel beside the viewport, so the model can be judged against the real animal rather than against an idea of it.

D-011 — Sculpt organic forms; leave hard surfaces crisp

2026-09-20. After the parts are built, the soft ones are voxel-remeshed into one continuous skin, relaxed and shaded smooth. Machines, crates, furniture, held weapons and anything asked for in a blocky style skip it.

Why: the other half of the same instruction. A body made of a box, two cylinders and a sphere reads as exactly that however carefully it is placed; the seams are the problem, not the smoothing. Headless Blender cannot drive sculpt-mode brushes, but the voxel remesh is what a sculptor reaches for first anyway, and it runs from Python.

Considered and rejected: metaballs, which blend beautifully but are hard to control and come out soft rather than sculpted; and subdivision alone, which rounds the facets but leaves every part boundary visible, so it would have fixed half the complaint.

D-012 — Remesh by colour group, and transfer the weights across

2026-09-20. Parts are grouped by material and each group is remeshed separately; vertex groups are painted on before the remesh and transferred back onto the new skin afterwards with a Data Transfer modifier.

Why: two things a voxel remesh destroys are exactly the two things boneka depends on. It keeps one material, which would have turned a knight into a monochrome blob; grouping by colour keeps the sleeve line between a blue upper arm and a skin-coloured forearm, which is a real edge rather than an artefact. And it throws vertex groups away, which would have destroyed the exact rig (D-001) and forced a fall back to guessing from bone positions. Transferring them back is what lets a model be both sculpted and exactly rigged.

A third casualty is anything thin: a voxel grid swallows what is thinner than about two voxels. Rather than list those parts by hand they are measured and excluded, so a new recipe gets the protection without anyone remembering to ask.

D-013 — Describe a part by its cross-sections, not by a primitive

2026-09-20. loft() builds a part from a list of rings, each with its own centre, half-width and half-depth, laid perpendicular to the path through its neighbours. Torsos, limbs, necks, heads, feet, paws, wings and tails are all lofts now; primitives are kept for the things that really are primitives.

Why: Luqman looked at the first sculpt pass and said the models were still blocky and wanted them more natural. Rendering the silhouette showed exactly why: the torso was a box. A box with its corners filed off is still a box, and no amount of smoothing or remeshing changes that, because the smoothing can only round what the primitive already is. The fix had to be upstream of the sculpt pass, in what the parts are.

Cross-sections are also how a figure is constructed on paper — ribcage as an oval, waist narrowing between ribcage and pelvis, deltoid capping the shoulder — so the primitive matches the way the proportions were already being described.

Three rules came out of getting it wrong first:

  • Two masses must overlap for the remesh to blend them. Meeting at a plane leaves a crease.
  • A limb's topmost ring must be narrower than the body it enters, or it punches a plate out through the flank. A blending mass goes there instead: a deltoid on a person, a shoulder blade or haunch on a dog.
  • The figure stands in a slight A-pose, because the gap between arm and waist is most of what makes a standing figure read as a person rather than a slab, and that gap only exists if the arm is held off the body.
D-014 — Size the voxel grid per colour group

2026-09-20. Each remesh group gets a voxel size from its own members rather than from the whole model, with a floor of height/200 and anything below it excluded from the group instead.

Why: the tip of a dog's ear is a few millimetres across. Sizing one grid for the whole model let that one runt drag the voxel size down to a millimetre on a seventy-centimetre animal, which tripled the triangle count and stopped anything fusing — a grid that fine simply reproduces the parts it was given, crease and all. Per-group sizing fixed the density and the fusion in one change.

D-015 — A character is assembled from named pieces, not coloured in

2026-09-20. Each kind of character has a kit: an ordered list of modules — breastplate, pauldrons, gauntlets, belt, tassets, greaves, boots, helmet, crest for a knight — and each module is its own part, fitted to the body underneath and tied to the bone it should move with.

Why: Luqman's words were "when i ask to make a 'knight' i want u to create the model 1 by 1 as a modular model, for example u create a base body first, then u make the helmet, then u make the armor and so on. it applies to any other models." Until then a knight was a blue person holding a sword: nothing about it was knightly, and the build had nothing to show beyond limbs.

Three things follow from doing it as modules rather than as one shape:

  • It animates correctly for free. The pauldron is attached to the shoulder bone, the greave to the shin, the helmet to the head, so the armour moves with the body without anything extra.
  • Every module is a function of the body it dresses, so the same helmet fits a tall thin figure and a short fat one, and a new character is a list of module names rather than new geometry.
  • The build is legible. The app names the pass it is on — Body, Face, Armour, Clothing, Gear — so the order is something to watch rather than something to take on trust. The belt goes on before the tassets because that is the order it goes on.

Modules are always hard: the sculpt pass never remeshes them into the body. A plate melted into a chest is not a plate.

D-016 — An unpainted character wears its own colours

2026-09-20. KIT_COLOURS gives each kit a default pair, used only when the prompt names no colour: a knight in steel, a ninja in black, a king in red and gold, a farmer in green and brown.

Why: every character was coming out the same blue, because the default body colour was blue and the armour took it. A colour word in the prompt still wins — a green knight is green — so nothing is taken away, and the common case stops needing a colour word at all.

Whatever a kit covers becomes an under-layer in a darker shade, so a garment reads as a garment on top of a body rather than as a differently shaped body.

D-017 — One palette for the whole model, matched in Oklab

2026-09-20. A prompt can name a palette; every colour on the model is then snapped onto it, shades included. Ten moods are built in and need no network; any palette on Lospec can be named and is cached on disk after the first fetch.

Why: Luqman asked whether boneka had access to different colour palettes. It had thirty-five colour words, raw hex, and shades derived by multiplying the body colour — which is why the dark under-layer often came out muddy rather than chosen. A real palette is a set of colours picked to sit together, and snapping the whole model onto one gets that coherence for nothing.

Matching is in Oklab, not RGB. Nearest-in-RGB is the obvious approach and it is wrong: it will swap a mid green for a dark blue because the numbers are close, while the eye sees nothing in common.

The network stays in the server, which resolves a name to a list of colours and hands Blender the colours. The worker never reaches out, so a build cannot hang on a slow request, and a palette used once keeps working offline.

D-018 — Snapping is an ordered assignment, solved exactly

2026-09-20. The model's colours are sorted by lightness, the palette is sorted by lightness, and each colour takes a strictly later swatch than the one before it, chosen to minimise the total colour difference — a shortest path over a grid.

Why: two properties have to hold at once and they pull against each other. Colours must stay distinct, or the model goes flat; and they must stay in order, or a shade that was darker than its base ends up lighter and the shading inverts, which reads as the form turning inside out.

Two greedier versions were written and both failed, which is the argument for doing it properly:

  • nearest swatch, then fix the shading — the fix-up pass kept undoing the de-duplication and putting two colours back on the same swatch
  • one greedy pass in lightness order — an early dark colour would take a light swatch, and everything after it had to be lighter still until the palette ran out

Sorting both lists and forcing a strictly increasing walk makes both properties true by construction; all that is left is which swatches to skip, and that is a small dynamic program. A palette smaller than the model shares swatches rather than distorting, and still never inverts.

D-019 — Texturelabs is the texture library, used as a multiply

2026-09-20. Luqman said "take this website as your textures library" of <https://texturelabs.org/>;. Every part now gets a surface from what it is, and the texture is multiplied over the colour the part already has rather than replacing it.

Why multiply: these are photographs for graphic design, not PBR material sets — there is no normal or roughness map anywhere on the site. Used as an albedo they would replace the colour and undo the palette work (D-017). Used as a multiply they do the one thing that was missing, which is to stop every surface being a flat slab, while the palette keeps deciding the colour.

Each photograph is reduced once to a grey detail map — desaturated, auto-contrasted, then squeezed into a narrow range around white — with the range set per surface, because skin and cloth want a whisper where rusted metal and bare soil can take a shove.

The ones used are hand-picked, not taken at random. The site holds design overlays and decorative tilework beside the real surfaces: the first random "brick" was Moroccan zellij and one "fabric" was a photograph of a t-shirt on a white background.

Same split as the palettes and the reference images: the server fetches and prepares, Blender is handed file paths. The worker never makes a network call, so a build cannot hang on a slow request.

D-020 — A textured export carries its own licence note

2026-09-20. Every .glb or .fbx with a Texturelabs texture in it gets a …_TEXTURES.txt written beside it, naming what is inside and repeating what may and may not be done with it. textures/ is gitignored.

Why: the terms are specific and they bite exactly here. Texturelabs resources "cannot ... be distributed or sold as part of a 3D model in a way that allows a third party to use, download, extract or access the Texturelabs asset" and cannot be "bundled with a 3D or graphics program, tool, app, or 3D engine". Using one inside a finished game is explicitly allowed; handing someone the model file is not, and committing the files to a repository is both redistribution and bundling.

Luqman chose this over the alternative of a separate "safe to share" export that strips textures — the note keeps the workflow to one button and keeps the obligation attached to the file rather than to anyone's memory.

D-021 — UVs are made last, and fitted rather than tiled

2026-09-20. Every part is smart-projected with scale_to_bounds, after the sculpt pass.

Why: a model has no UVs to start with — the lofts are built with bmesh, which makes none, and a voxel remesh throws away whatever a mesh had — so there is no point unwrapping any earlier. Fitting the texture once across each part rather than repeating it is because these are photographs, not tiling materials: repeating one would show the seam every time.

D-022 — Light and material before geometry

2026-09-20. Asked for "complex modelling until the output become realistic", the order chosen was materials and lighting first, geometry after.

Why: a surface looks real when the light on it varies, not when the colour does. Flat colour with a photograph multiplied over it still read as painted plastic, because the colour varied and the specular did not. Deriving a normal and a roughness map from the same photograph, and lighting the scene with a Poly Haven HDRI instead of three lamps in a void, changed the output more than any geometry work in the same time.

Neither map is a real measurement. A height map guessed from a photograph's brightness reads a dark stain as a dent. It is still far better than the constants that were there before, and tools/pbr.py says so in its own docstring rather than pretending otherwise.

D-023 — Garments go through the sculpt pass after all

2026-09-20. Reverses part of D-011 and D-015, which kept all clothing and armour out of the sculpt pass to preserve their edges.

Why: left out of it they stayed as raw intersecting lofts, and every loft is capped flat — so a sleeve ended in a flat disc hanging in mid-air over the arm, and under the new lighting those caps caught the light and were the first thing the eye went to.

What actually keeps a garment from melting into the body is not being exempt from the sculpt pass, it is being a different colour: the pass fuses one colour group at a time, and a garment's colour is never the under-layer's. So the garments now fuse with each other into one continuous piece of clothing and still cannot touch the body. The small hard details — visor, buckle, crest, eyes, sword blade — stay exempt, because a remesh would swallow them.

The check was rewritten to assert the property that actually matters (a garment's colour differs from the body's) instead of the rule it replaced.

D-024 — The face is where the procedural approach stops

2026-09-20. face_parts builds a brow, a nose ridge, ears, a jaw and one small dark eye each side. Cheekbones, lips and a white-with-an-iris eye were built, photographed and removed.

Why: a face is the one shape everybody knows by heart, and overlapping ellipsoids fused together will not make one. Three versions were built and looked at:

  • true size — every feature vanished into the skull during the relax
  • enlarged to survive it — the features stopped being features and became a lump under the jaw that read as a beak
  • a white eye with an iris — reads as a ping-pong ball glued to the cheek; at this scale an eye is four millimetres of surface and there is no room in it for two parts

One version placed both eyes entirely inside the head, because the skull narrows away from its centre line and an eye put at the head's nominal depth ends up behind the surface.

This is recorded as a limit, not a to-do. Anything better needs a different method: a hand-modelled head library to graft on, or Meshy, which Luqman has chosen to leave alone for now.

D-025 — A local open-weight generator, on the PC, not on the Mac

2026-09-20. Luqman asked whether boneka could be like Meshy and Tripo. It cannot be trained into one, but it does not have to be: Tencent's Hunyuan3D-2 is open-weight and does the same job. It goes on the Windows PC at the academy and is reached over Tailscale.

Why the PC: this MacBook Air is an M2 with 8 GB of unified memory, shared with macOS, Blender and the browser. These models are CUDA-first and the smallest useful one wants 5 GB to itself. The RTX 3060 has 12 GB, which is comfortable for shape generation and right at the edge for shape plus texture — so the install is staged, shape first with nothing to compile, texture second behind a flag.

Why not just use Meshy: Meshy is better, especially on texture, and he has credits. But it costs money per model and needs the network, and this is free, unlimited and offline once installed. The two are not exclusive; both land in the same import path.

What it does not change: a generated mesh is one lump with no named parts, so it uses the fitted rig, not the exact one. The exact rig depends on boneka having built the mesh itself and knowing what every part is (D-001), and no generator can give that back.

Untested. It was written while the PC answered a Tailscale ping at 21 ms but had nothing listening — no claude-chat, no SSH — so there was no way to get a shell. Every call from the Mac fails quickly with a plain reason instead of hanging, and the health check says which of "not set up" and "not answering" is true.

D-026 — A loft can extrude a polygon, not only an ellipse

2026-09-20. ring() takes an optional profile: a closed polygon in unit space, scaled by the ring's two radii. GABLE and SQUARE are provided.

Why: Luqman sent a picture of boneka's house and said it was not one. It was not: a 3.2 x 2.6 x 1.2 m slab with a four-sided pyramid standing in the middle of its roof, the pyramid's base sitting below the top of the walls so it read as a hat resting inside a box.

A house needs a gabled roof, and a gable is a triangle run along a ridge — which is exactly a loft with a triangular section, capped at both ends. So the primitive was generalised rather than the roof being faked out of rotated boxes. Anything with a constant non-round section can use it now.

One thing had to be fixed to make profiles usable at all: the ring's frame took its second axis as tangent x axis_x, which points down for a horizontal run. For an ellipse that is invisible, because an ellipse is symmetric; for a triangle it builds the roof underground. It is now axis_x x tangent, so a profile's "up" is up.

D-027 — Eaves are what make a building a building

2026-09-20. The house is rebuilt at real dimensions — about 7 m across, 5.5 m deep, 2.7 m to the eaves, a thirty-degree gable, 40 cm of overhang all round — with a foundation course, framed windows with sills and bars, a framed door with a handle and a step, and a chimney that comes up through the roof.

Why those three things in particular: the roof has to be gabled, it has to overhang, and it has to start at the wall top. A roof flush with the wall is a shed; a roof that starts inside the wall is a hat. The overhang is the single detail doing the most work, and it cost one number.

The checks now assert the shape rather than the parts list: the roof starts within a quarter of the building's height of the wall top, it is wider and deeper than the walls, its peak is above them, and the walls are over a metre tall.

  • the cape still a flat plank; now a curved shell
  • the chicken's tail standing off its back as a separate white card, because it was in the light colour and so fell into a different remesh group; it is now body-coloured and fuses
  • a 20 cm chicken being remeshed at 1.8 mm, because the voxel bounds were the wrong way round — min(slimmest/3, height/70) is what protects the hands without drowning a small model

673 checks green in 28 seconds.

Third pass the same day: "the model is still blocky, make it more natural"

A silhouette render settled it in one picture, and it is the technique worth keeping: a flat black shape against white, no lighting to flatter it. The torso was a box. A rounded slab with square shoulder corners, arms welded to its sides, two identical sticks for legs and no waist at all. Smoothing a box gives a smooth box — the problem was upstream of the sculpt pass, in what the parts were made of.

The fix: a new primitive

loft() describes a part by its cross-sections: a list of rings, each with its own centre, half-width and half-depth, laid perpendicular to the path through its neighbours. Everything organic is a loft now — torsos, limbs, necks, heads, feet, paws, wings, tails.

  • The torso is a stack of rings: crotch, hip crest, waist (the narrowest), lower ribs, chest, shoulder line, trapezius, neck base. Split at the waist so the pelvis and the ribcage each carry their own bone, sharing their middle rings so the overlap has no step in it.
  • An arm is a shoulder ring, a bicep swell, an elbow, a forearm swell and a wrist. A leg is hip, thigh mass, knee, calf belly, ankle.
  • Widths came from anthropometry, not the drawing canon: biacromial breadth about 0.234 H, shoulder-to-hip about 1.4. The canon's 2⅓ heads measures the fleshed silhouette, so the skeletal ring is narrower and the deltoids make up the difference — which is exactly the shape that was missing.
  • The dog got the same: a deep chest tucking to a waist, the chest reaching halfway down the leg, and an angulated hind leg with the stifle forward and the hock back.
  • The chicken got a body that tapers from breast to tail with the wing as a lens on the flank.

Three rules, each learned by breaking it

  • Two masses must overlap to blend. Meeting at a plane leaves a crease.
  • A limb's top ring must be narrower than the body it enters, or it punches a plate out through the flank. That plate was the thing that kept reading as "blocky" on the dog through three attempts to fix it elsewhere.
  • Leave air under the arm. The gap between arm and waist is most of what makes a standing figure read as a person, so the rest pose is a slight A-pose.

And one in the sculpt pass

The voxel grid is now sized per colour group. One tiny part — the tip of a dog's ear — was dragging the global voxel size down to a millimetre on a seventy-centimetre animal, which tripled the triangle count and stopped anything fusing, because a grid that fine just reproduces the parts it was given.

673 checks still green, and the walk cycle still deforms correctly over the new anatomy with the sword staying in the hand.

Fourth pass: "create the model 1 by 1 as a modular model"

"for example u create a base body first, then u make the helmet, then u make the armor and so on. it applies to any other models."

Until this, a knight was a blue person holding a sword. Nothing about it was knightly, and the build had nothing to show beyond limbs appearing.

Kits

Each kind of character now has a kit: an ordered list of the pieces it is assembled from. A knight is breastplate, pauldrons, gauntlets, belt, tassets, greaves, boots, helmet, crest. Twenty-six modules cover about thirty characters — knight, wizard, pirate, ninja, king, farmer, chef, viking, astronaut, soldier, orc, footballer and the rest — and anyone with no kit of their own still gets a tunic, trousers and boots, because a plain person wearing nothing looks like an oversight.

Three things fall out of building them as modules:

  • They animate for free. Each piece is attached to the bone it belongs to, so the pauldron turns with the shoulder and the greave swings with the shin. The armoured knight rigs to the same 18 bones, exactly, and walks.
  • Each module is a function of the body it dresses, so the same helmet fits a tall thin figure and a short fat one, and a new character is a list of names rather than new geometry.
  • The build is legible. The app names the pass it is on — Body, Face, Armour, Clothing, Gear — and the belt goes on before the tassets because that is the order it goes on.

Colours

Every character was coming out the same blue, because the default body colour was blue and the armour took it. Each kit now carries its own pair, used only when the prompt names none: steel for a knight, black for a ninja, red and gold for a king, green and brown for a farmer. A colour word still wins — a green knight is green. And whatever a kit covers becomes an under-layer in a darker shade, so a garment reads as a garment on a body rather than as a differently shaped body.

Fixed on the second look

The helmet was a beanie with a mast on top; it now covers the skull and the plume is a crest running front to back. The hats were saucers; the brims came in and the crowns went up. Tunics had no sleeves. The hood swallowed the whole face, and is now pushed back with a cowl over the shoulders.

722 checks — including that a knight has a helmet, a golem wears nothing, an unpainted knight is steel, a green one is green, and every piece hangs off a bone that actually exists.

Fifth pass: palettes

"do u have access to different color palette?"

It had thirty-five colour words, raw hex, and shades made by multiplying the body colour — which is why the dark under-layer so often came out muddy rather than chosen. Asked how far to take it, he picked real palettes.

Now a prompt or a field in the app can name one, and every colour on the model comes from it, shades included. Ten moods are built in and need no network — pastel, muted, earthy, neon, monochrome, sunset, forest, ice, rust, candy — and any palette on Lospec can be named by slug. Fetched palettes are cached on disk, so one used once keeps working offline. The server resolves the name and hands Blender the colours, so the worker never makes a network call and a build cannot hang on a slow request.

The snapping was the interesting part

Matching is in Oklab. Nearest-in-RGB is the obvious approach and it is wrong — it will swap a mid green for a dark blue because the numbers are close.

Two properties have to hold at once and they pull against each other. Colours must stay distinct, or the model goes flat. And they must stay in lightness order, or a shade that was darker than its base ends up lighter and the form reads inside out.

Two greedy versions were written and both failed, which is worth recording:

  1. nearest swatch, then fix the shading. The fix-up pass kept undoing the de-duplication and putting two colours back on the same swatch.
  2. one greedy pass in lightness order. An early dark colour would take a light swatch, and everything after it had to be lighter still until the palette ran out — nine of the ten moods failed the distinctness check.

The version that works sorts both lists by lightness and forces a strictly increasing walk through the palette, which makes both properties true by construction. All that is left is which swatches to skip, and that is a small dynamic program over (colour, swatch) minimising total colour difference. A palette smaller than the model shares swatches rather than distorting, and still never inverts — checked with a three-colour palette.

765 checks, including every mood plus a real Lospec palette asserted for both properties.

Sixth pass: textures from Texturelabs

Luqman pasted <https://texturelabs.org/>; and said "take this website as your textures library".

The licence came first

It is free, commercial-safe and asks for no credit, but two clauses bite exactly on 3D work. Texturelabs resources cannot "be distributed or sold as part of a 3D model in a way that allows a third party to use, download, extract or access" them, and cannot "be bundled with a 3D or graphics program, tool, app, or 3D engine". Using one inside a finished game is explicitly allowed.

So: textures/ is gitignored, and every textured export writes a …_TEXTURES.txt beside it naming what is inside and repeating the restriction. Asked how to handle it, he chose that over a second "safe to share" export button.

A CLI first, then boneka

texturelabs went on the PATH next to polyhaven and sfx — categories, search, list, get, pick, sync — because the library is useful outside boneka. The catalogue comes from the site's own sitemaps: 2,035 textures in 18 categories, and the slug prefix is the category. Only the "S" file is served publicly, and it turned out to be 1920 px, not the 960 the site advertises.

How they are used

As a multiply over the colour a part already has, never as a replacement. These are photographs for graphic design — there is no normal or roughness map anywhere on the site — so used as an albedo they would undo the palette work. Each is reduced once to a grey detail map and squeezed into a narrow range around white, per surface: a whisper for skin and cloth, a shove for rusted metal and bare soil.

Which surface a part gets is decided by its name, centrally, rather than by tagging a hundred call sites — with a per-prop fallback for parts named too generically to read, like a barrel's "body".

Three things that went wrong

  • ShaderNodeMixRGB is silently ignored by Blender 5's glTF exporter. The colour vanished and every material exported as plain grey, with no error. The modern ShaderNodeMix exports as baseColorFactor × baseColorTexture correctly. Found by exporting four test cubes, one per node shape, and reading the glTF JSON.
  • A prop word in a prompt is not the subject's material. "a knight with a sword" sets prop to sword, which briefly made the whole knight metal.
  • A tree is mostly wood, but its leaves are not planks. The canopy came out with wood grain until foliage was named explicitly.

Picking textures at random was tried and abandoned: the first random "brick" was Moroccan zellij and one "fabric" was a photograph of a t-shirt on white. Forty candidates were downloaded and looked at, and the good ones hardcoded.

779 checks.

Eighth pass: "this is not a house"

He sent a screenshot. He was right: a squat slab with a pyramid standing in the middle of its roof — and the pyramid's base was below the top of the walls, so it read as a hat resting inside a box rather than a roof on a building.

The fix needed a new primitive. A house wants a gabled roof, which is a triangle run along a ridge, so ring() learned to take a polygon profile instead of only an ellipse; a gable is then a two-ring loft, capped at both ends, which gives the two gable walls for free.

That exposed a bug that had been harmless until now. The ring's frame took its second axis as tangent x axis_x, which points down for a horizontal run. Invisible on an ellipse, because an ellipse is symmetric. On a triangle it builds the roof underground.

The house itself is now at real dimensions — 7 m across, 5.5 m deep, 2.7 m to the eaves, thirty-degree pitch, 40 cm overhang — with a foundation course, a framed door with a handle and a step, framed windows with sills and bars, a ridge cap and a chimney that comes up through the roof. Of all of it the overhang does the most work: a roof flush with the wall is a shed.

One bug on the second look: the gable triangles poked through the roof slope and showed as slivers of wall on the tiles, because they sat at the same height and pitch as the roof rather than just under it.

783 checks, including that the roof starts at the top of the walls rather than inside them.
